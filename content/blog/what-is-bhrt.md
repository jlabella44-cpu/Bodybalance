If you've been Googling "why am I so tired all the time" or "is this what menopause feels like," there's a decent chance you've stumbled across the term BHRT. It's everywhere right now — but the information is inconsistent, confusing, and often shaped more by marketing than medicine.

Let's cut through the noise.

## What Does "Bioidentical" Actually Mean?

Bioidentical hormones are compounds that are chemically identical in structure to the hormones your body naturally produces. That's the key distinction from synthetic hormones, which are similar but not identical — and that structural difference matters when it comes to how your body's receptors respond.

The most commonly used bioidentical hormones include:

- **Estradiol (E2)** — the primary estrogen used in BHRT for women
- **Progesterone** — used to balance estrogen and support sleep and mood
- **Testosterone** — prescribed for both women and men at different doses
- **DHEA** — a precursor hormone that declines with age

Some of these are FDA-approved (like certain estradiol patches and micronized progesterone capsules). Others are custom-compounded by licensed pharmacies to match a specific patient's prescription.

## Why Do People Seek It Out?

The most common reasons patients come to us asking about BHRT:

**Women** are often experiencing perimenopause or menopause symptoms — hot flashes, night sweats, poor sleep, brain fog, mood changes, low libido, vaginal dryness, and weight gain that didn't respond to diet and exercise. Many were told by their conventional doctor that their labs are "normal" and there's nothing to do but wait it out.

**Men** are often dealing with what they might call "just getting older" — fatigue that doesn't improve with rest, reduced motivation and drive, increasing body fat (especially around the midsection), difficulty building or maintaining muscle, and a libido that's quietly disappeared. These are classic signs of testosterone decline, which typically starts in a man's 30s and accelerates after 40.

Both groups share a common thread: symptoms that are real, documented, and impairing quality of life — but that often get dismissed in a 10-minute primary care visit.

## What Does Treatment Actually Look Like?

Before anyone at Body Balance receives a single prescription, we run a comprehensive lab panel. There's no responsible way to prescribe hormones without knowing your baseline levels, and frankly, symptoms alone aren't enough to guide a precise protocol.

Once we have your labs, we sit down and walk through every marker together. Then we build a protocol specific to your biology — not a one-size-fits-all dose based on your age or gender.

Delivery methods vary by protocol and patient preference:

- **Pellets** — inserted under the skin every 3–5 months; provides consistent, steady hormone levels without daily adherence
- **Topical creams or gels** — applied daily; good for dose flexibility
- **Injections** — typically self-administered weekly; very effective for testosterone
- **Oral capsules** — most common for progesterone

## What Does the Evidence Say?

This is where we want to be straightforward with you: BHRT has a significant and growing body of evidence behind it, but it is not without nuance.

For women, the landmark **Women's Health Initiative (WHI) study** in 2002 scared a generation away from HRT after finding elevated risks of breast cancer and cardiovascular disease. But here's the critical context: that study used synthetic, not bioidentical, hormones — and it studied a population of women who were, on average, 63 years old and well past the hormonal transition window.

Subsequent research, including meta-analyses of bioidentical estradiol and progesterone, has shown a substantially more favorable safety profile — particularly when therapy is initiated within 10 years of menopause onset (the so-called "timing hypothesis").

For men, the evidence supporting testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) for symptomatic hypogonadism is robust. The **TRAVERSE trial**, published in 2023, was the largest cardiovascular safety study of TRT to date and found no increased risk of major cardiac events in men with or at risk for heart disease.

None of this means BHRT is risk-free or right for everyone. There are contraindications, and individual risk assessment matters enormously. Which is why we start with labs and a thorough conversation — not a prescription pad.
