One of the most common things women say when they come to Body Balance is: "I didn't think I was old enough for menopause."

They're often right. They're not in menopause. They're in **perimenopause** — the transitional phase that can begin anywhere from a woman's mid-30s to mid-40s, last anywhere from 4 to 10 years, and produce symptoms that are just as disruptive as menopause itself.

The distinction matters — both for understanding what you're experiencing and for treatment.

## What Is Perimenopause?

Menopause is a specific clinical milestone: 12 consecutive months without a menstrual period. The average age in the U.S. is 51.

**Perimenopause** is everything leading up to that point. It's the years (sometimes a decade) during which your ovaries gradually produce less estrogen and progesterone, and the hormonal rhythm that has regulated your cycle, mood, sleep, and cognition since puberty begins to fluctuate and wind down.

The key word is *fluctuate*. Perimenopause isn't a smooth descent — it's erratic. Estrogen can spike and crash unpredictably. Progesterone tends to decline earlier. Testosterone drops too, often with less attention than it deserves. This hormonal volatility is what drives most of the symptoms women experience.

## Common Perimenopause Symptoms (Many of Which Get Missed)

**Sleep disruption** is among the earliest and most impactful symptoms. This often manifests as difficulty staying asleep, waking in the early morning hours, or waking drenched in sweat — even before hot flashes become obvious during the day.

**Mood changes** — anxiety, irritability, emotional reactivity, or a sense of feeling "not yourself" — are extremely common and often misattributed to stress, depression, or relationship problems. The hormonal contribution is frequently missed.

**Brain fog and memory changes** are reported by up to 60% of perimenopausal women. Difficulty concentrating, word-finding issues, and short-term memory lapses are all associated with declining estrogen, which plays a key role in neurological function.

**Irregular cycles** are usually the clearest signal, but women often experience the symptoms above for months before their cycle becomes noticeably irregular.

**Hot flashes and night sweats** — the "classic" menopause symptoms — can begin in perimenopause, though they often intensify around the menopausal transition itself.

**Low libido, vaginal dryness, and changes in sexual function** — estrogen and testosterone both play roles here, and both decline during the perimenopause transition.

**Weight changes** — particularly increased fat storage around the abdomen — are related to both hormonal changes and shifts in insulin sensitivity that accompany the transition.

## Why This Gets Missed

A 35 or 40-year-old woman going to her OB-GYN with fatigue, poor sleep, and anxiety is likely to be told she's stressed and possibly offered an antidepressant or sleep medication. Without specific hormonal testing — and without a clinician who knows what to look for — the hormonal dimension stays invisible.

Standard TSH and basic metabolic panels don't capture the hormonal picture. A thorough assessment should include estradiol, progesterone, testosterone, SHBG, FSH, and LH — plus thyroid markers, which become more volatile in the perimenopause years and produce overlapping symptoms.

## What Can Be Done?

The honest answer is: quite a bit. The goal isn't to prevent aging — it's to support your body through a transition that medicine can genuinely help with.

**Bioidentical hormone therapy (BHRT)** — using estradiol, progesterone, and often low-dose testosterone — can significantly improve sleep, mood, cognition, libido, and vasomotor symptoms (hot flashes, night sweats). The evidence for early initiation is substantially more favorable than many women have been told, and the risk-benefit calculation looks different for bioidentical hormones than for the synthetic hormones studied in older research.

**Lifestyle optimization** remains foundational — strength training preserves muscle mass and insulin sensitivity, protein intake matters more than most women realize during this transition, and sleep hygiene becomes especially important when sleep is already being disrupted.

**Hormonal support isn't a magic fix**, but for women experiencing significant symptoms, it's also not optional to just "push through." This transition has real biological underpinnings, and treating it as such is entirely appropriate.
